Long haul truckers spend thousands of hours with one side of the face closer to glass. Windshields are usually laminated and block most UVB and a large share of UVA, but side windows may allow more UVA through depending on the glass and tint. UVA is important because it penetrates deeper than UVB, contributes to photoaging, and is associated with skin cancer risk over time.

A face cream can support the skin barrier and help with dryness, dullness, and uneven-looking tone, but it should not be treated as sun protection unless it is labeled as a sunscreen with an SPF and broad-spectrum claim. For drivers, the most evidence-based daily step is broad-spectrum sunscreen on exposed skin, especially the face, ears, neck, forearms, and hands.

What dermatology evidence says about niacinamide

Niacinamide, a form of vitamin B3, has better topical skincare evidence than many trend ingredients. In cosmetic use, it is commonly studied for supporting the skin barrier, reducing transepidermal water loss, improving the look of uneven tone, and helping skin feel calmer. It is not the same as prescription skin cancer prevention, and it should not replace sunscreen, protective clothing, or dermatology checkups.

For truckers, niacinamide can be practical because cab air, irregular sleep, heat, cold, and frequent washing can make skin feel dry or stressed. A well-formulated moisturizer with niacinamide may be useful in a morning or night routine, especially if shaving, wind, or dry air causes tightness.

It is important not to blur those lines. Niacinamide can be part of skincare; it is not a shield against UVA.

Where NAD+ face cream fits, and where evidence is early

NAD+ is involved in cellular energy metabolism and repair pathways inside the body, which is why it has attracted interest in aging research. Topical NAD+ skincare, however, is still an early evidence area. There is a plausible scientific rationale, but there is not enough dermatology evidence to claim that an NAD+ face cream prevents skin cancer, reverses sun damage, or produces medical outcomes.

When evaluating any NAD face cream, look for the full formula rather than a single headline ingredient. A useful driver-friendly moisturizer should be comfortable under sunscreen, non-greasy enough for daytime wear, and supportive of the skin barrier. Ingredients such as niacinamide, humectants, and emollients can make a formula more practical for daily use.

A practical cab routine for UVA-heavy days

  • Apply broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher before driving, including the left side of the face, ears, neck, hands, and forearms.
  • Reapply sunscreen during long shifts, especially after sweating, wiping the face, or many hours behind glass.
  • Use UV-protective sunglasses and consider UPF sleeves or clothing for the window-side arm.
  • Ask about legal UV-filtering window film where you drive; rules vary by location.
  • Book regular skin checks, and see a dermatologist promptly for changing, bleeding, painful, or non-healing spots.

At night, cleanse off sunscreen, road dust, and sweat without over-scrubbing. Then use a moisturizer to reduce dryness and support the barrier. If you are adding active ingredients and you have a history of skin cancer, precancerous lesions, severe sensitivity, or a diagnosed skin condition, check with a dermatologist first.
