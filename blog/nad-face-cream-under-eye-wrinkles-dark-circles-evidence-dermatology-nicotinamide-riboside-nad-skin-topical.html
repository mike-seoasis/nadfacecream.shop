The skin around the eyes is thin, low in oil glands, and more likely to show dehydration, fine lines, puffiness, and shadowing. “Under-eye wrinkles” can mean expression lines, crepey texture, dryness lines, or sun-related collagen change. “Dark circles” can come from pigment, visible blood vessels, hollowness, swelling, allergies, sleep disruption, or genetics. A face cream may help the surface look smoother and more hydrated, but it cannot correct every cause.

NAD+ is a molecule involved in cellular energy metabolism and repair pathways. Nicotinamide riboside is a vitamin B3-related compound studied as an NAD+ precursor, mostly in nutrition and cellular biology contexts. In skincare, the idea is that supporting skin’s energy-related processes may be useful for visible aging, but topical NAD+ and topical nicotinamide riboside evidence is still early. It is not the same level of evidence as long-established dermatology ingredients such as sunscreen, retinoids, or some forms of vitamin B3 used for barrier support.

What dermatology evidence can and cannot say

Dermatology evidence for the under-eye area usually focuses on measurable outcomes: hydration, barrier function, irritation, pigment appearance, wrinkle depth, elasticity, and tolerability. For NAD+ topical skincare, the most responsible position is cautious: there is scientific rationale, but fewer large, independent human studies specifically showing under-eye wrinkle or dark-circle improvement from topical NAD+ or nicotinamide riboside.

Nicotinamide riboside as a precursor is another. Whether either ingredient remains stable in a cream, reaches relevant skin layers, and creates visible cosmetic change around the eyes is a formulation and clinical-testing question. A good product should not rely on ingredient buzz alone; it should also be gentle, moisturizing, and suitable for daily use.

What to look for in an under-eye-friendly face cream

Because the eye area is reactive, the base formula matters as much as the active story. A useful cream for fine lines should reduce dryness, support the skin barrier, and wear comfortably without stinging. Hydrated skin reflects light more evenly, so dehydration lines and dull shadowing may look softer even when deeper causes remain.

  • Look for humectants that bind water, such as glycerin or similar hydrating ingredients.
  • Look for barrier-supporting emollients that reduce roughness and tightness.
  • Avoid applying strong acids, high-strength retinoids, or fragranced formulas close to the lash line unless directed by a professional.
  • Use sunscreen in the daytime, because UV exposure worsens wrinkles and some forms of discoloration.
  • Patch test first if your eye area stings easily or you have a history of sensitivity.

Apply a small amount along the orbital bone, not directly into the eye. More product is not better; migration into the eyes can cause watering, which makes the area look puffier and more irritated. If dark circles are mainly caused by hollowness or anatomy, a cream can improve texture and hydration but will not replace medical or procedural options.

How to set realistic expectations

For wrinkles, expect the most visible cosmetic changes from improved hydration, smoother texture, and better light reflection. For dark circles, improvement depends on the cause. Brownish discoloration may need strict sun protection and pigment-focused care. Bluish or purple tones may be related to vascular visibility or thin skin. Morning puffiness may be linked to fluid retention, sleep position, salt intake, allergies, or irritation.

It is reasonable to be interested, but unreasonable to expect a cream to erase wrinkles, diagnose dark circles, or act like a medical treatment.

Near the eyes, consistency and tolerance matter. Introduce one new active product at a time, use it for several weeks, and stop if you develop persistent burning, swelling, rash, or worsening discoloration. If dark circles change suddenly, appear with swelling, or are linked with eczema, allergies, or other medical concerns, check with a dermatologist before adding new actives.
