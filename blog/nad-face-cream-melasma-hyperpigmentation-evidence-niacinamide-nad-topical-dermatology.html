NAD face cream melasma hyperpigmentation evidence niacinamide NAD topical dermatology

Melasma and hyperpigmentation are not just “uneven tone.” Melasma is a medical pigment condition often influenced by ultraviolet light, visible light, hormones, heat, and inflammation. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation can follow acne, irritation, procedures, or rashes. Because the causes differ, a face cream should not be positioned as a treatment or cure.

The most useful skincare approach is usually supportive: reduce avoidable triggers, protect the skin barrier, avoid irritation, and choose actives with realistic expectations. Anyone with melasma, rapidly changing pigmentation, pregnancy-related pigment changes, or darker patches that are new or spreading should check with a dermatologist before adding new actives.

What the evidence says about niacinamide

Niacinamide has more dermatology relevance than topical NAD+ at this point. It is a form of vitamin B3 used in skincare for barrier support, redness-prone skin comfort, oiliness, and the appearance of uneven tone. In pigmentation routines, niacinamide is often discussed because it may help reduce the look of discoloration by influencing how pigment is transferred within the skin.

That does not mean niacinamide works like a prescription melasma therapy. Results, when they occur, are gradual and depend on the person, the type of pigmentation, the formula, and sun exposure. It is generally considered a steady supportive ingredient rather than a fast dark-spot remover.

For melasma and hyperpigmentation, consistency matters more than using many strong products. Over-exfoliating, layering too many acids, or combining multiple brightening actives without guidance can inflame the skin and make discoloration look worse, especially in deeper skin tones.

Where topical NAD+ fits, and where evidence is still early

NAD+ is involved in cellular energy and repair pathways, which is why it is discussed in aging and skin biology. However, topical NAD+ skincare evidence is still early compared with ingredients such as niacinamide, sunscreen filters, retinoids, azelaic acid, and certain dermatologist-directed options. It is reasonable to be interested in NAD+ face cream, but it should be viewed as a cosmetic support product, not a medical pigment treatment.

The phrase NAD face cream melasma hyperpigmentation evidence niacinamide NAD topical dermatology should therefore be interpreted cautiously: niacinamide has a clearer skincare track record, while NAD+ is an emerging topical ingredient with promising rationale but limited direct clinical proof for melasma or hyperpigmentation.

A practical NAD+ face cream formula for pigmentation-prone skin should focus on comfort and barrier support. If the formula stings, causes peeling, or leaves the skin tight, it may be counterproductive for anyone dealing with discoloration.

What to look for, what to avoid, and when to ask a dermatologist

For pigment-prone skin, daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is the non-negotiable step. Visible light can also matter for melasma, so many dermatologists recommend tinted mineral sunscreens with iron oxides, especially for stubborn patches. A face cream can support the routine, but it cannot replace photoprotection.

  • Look for barrier-supportive ingredients such as glycerin, ceramides, fatty alcohols, squalane, and niacinamide if tolerated.
  • Avoid harsh scrubs, frequent high-strength exfoliation, and fragranced products if your skin is easily irritated.
  • Patch test new products on a small area for several days before applying to the whole face.
  • Introduce only one new active at a time so you can tell what helps or irritates.
  • Ask a dermatologist before combining creams with prescription pigment treatments, peels, lasers, or microneedling.
